The engine room under the rest of this page. The music pipeline, the album art, and the 3D tooling all run on these; they are the parts you do not see, but everything else leans on them.

distributed systems

pullqueue

This is the queue that runs the GPU job lanes on my home server, the stem separation and cover art and voice and mesh jobs behind the music and 3D work above. Workers poll for jobs, a lease expires when a worker dies so the job gets served to the next one, and completion is arbitrated so it only happens once. It is packaged here as a standalone library, with a kill -9 crash-recovery demo and tests in CI. A lease-based version runs on a plain JSON file; an event-sourced version runs on Postgres when the history has to survive the process.

ML infrastructure

gpustack

The same 12 GB GPU that renders the album art and the 3D meshes also runs stem separation, several local LLMs, and a coding agent, and this is the arbitration layer that keeps them from fighting over memory. It coordinates through a file lock rather than a daemon, breaks the lock if the process holding it dies, and gates on memory pressure instead of hoping two models happen to fit.

AI engineering

The Agent Workshop

After my local-model assistants proved a consumer GPU could carry real work, I built co-hog, a coding agent that runs on my own 12 GB card so not every task has to lean on paid frontier tokens. The harness around it mattered more than the model inside it, and the thing it taught me was to check the harness first when an agent fails. The bigger campaigns, like porting five Minecraft mods, I ran by directing frontier agents instead, and every new kind of failure I hit there turned into a reusable diagnostic skill.

The Story

How wanting my own Spotify turned into all of this

I just wanted my own music instead of Spotify, and it got a little out of hand.

It started with setting up Navidrome, an open-source music server, to replace Spotify, and then I missed the playlists and the lyrics, so I built lyric fetching, transcription, and mood analysis. The mood playlists needed AI covers, and the image models lived on another machine's GPU, so I built a job queue, and after that the voices went on the queue, then the video, the 3D meshes, and a coding agent, until all the copies of it collapsed into one lease engine on an event-sourced Postgres log. It grew that far out of wanting the music nights at home to keep getting better.
